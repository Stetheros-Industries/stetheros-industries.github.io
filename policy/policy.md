# Stetheros Industries: Corporate Structure and Operating Policies

Oct 25, 2026

## Summary

Stetheros Industries is a member-run industrial company. Members make goods and deposit them with the company, and the company is the only seller. Members are paid a fixed share of every sale, can earn ownership (equity) and dividends, and share in running the company. The company operates in several nations, with Prunia as its home, and agrees terms with national governments that return value to them. Every amount in this document is in the Prunian Credit (the Credit).

**The company in ten points**

1. **One seller.** Only the company lists company goods for sale. Members produce goods and deposit them in company storage, and may not sell catalog goods, or goods made with company production infrastructure, anywhere else. A sale outside the company is handled by the three-strike system. With a 66% board vote, a member may run a branded division that sells under its own name while remaining part of the company in every other respect (§3, §3.8, §9.8).
2. **Revenue split.** When a member's goods sell, the member receives 80% of the revenue and the company keeps 20%. Where the company pays for approved supplies, the split applies to profit after those supplies. The split applies to the price after any discount, and a promotional discount on goods from a member's own production needs that member's consent (§3.7, §4.1, §4.2).
3. **Paid before the sale.** For goods with a proven sales record, meaning sales in at least 3 of the last 4 weeks, the company pays 20% of the member's cut when the goods are deposited and the rest when they sell. Goods without a record, and deposits beyond what the company will pay for up front, are accepted on consignment and paid only after they sell (§4.3 to §4.5).
4. **Stock targets.** The company aims to hold two weeks of expected sales of each good. Up-front payment is guaranteed only while stock is below that target, and the weekly budget is divided equally among active members, with a priority contract holder's committed quota covered first. A ledger kept by the Sales role and checked by an auditor records every deposit (§5).
5. **Ownership and dividends.** Members earn equity points by contributing assets, with a bonus for contributing early in a server's life. The company distributes part of its profit to active members. The rate is 25% at server launch, and afterward the CEO or the board may change it, subject to the usual board challenge. Of each dividend, 85% is paid by equity share and 15% equally among active members, and a new member without equity starts receiving the equal portion after two weeks. A member stays active by passing the activity test every two weeks, inactive points decay, and a member who leaves is bought out at the original value of the equity granted (§6).
6. **Assets.** A member keeps an asset by default and sells its goods through the company at the 80/20 split. The company buys an asset only with approval, at a value fixed on approval, paying half at purchase and the rest from what the asset earns. The member may take payment as equity, cash, or goods, and builders of infrastructure also receive weekly payments while it serves the company (§7).
7. **Contracts and services.** The company may give a member a priority contract, under which the member's goods are drawn first for sale in return for a weekly quota. Goods outside the catalog are unrestricted, custom orders have their own rules, and the company sells services on members' behalf, keeping 20% (§8).
8. **Governance.** A CEO and a board run the company. The CEO can approve builds, contracts, and supply budgets alone, but a majority of the board can challenge an approval, and an approval becomes final when its 24-hour challenge window closes. Each eligible member has one vote, not weighted by equity, and the CEO can be removed by 66% of eligible members together with a majority of equity. Rule-breaking is handled by a three-strike system, with removal at the fourth strike (§9).
9. **Reimbursement.** A member who pays with personal money for an approved company purchase, because no board member is online, is repaid within 7 days. A late payment carries a premium of 10% of the claim for each full week, up to 30% (§10).
10. **Nations and war.** Prunia is covered by a Home Charter, and any other nation may sign the Stetheros Compact. Governments and citizens of both receive a 10% discount on company goods, and both share in a profit pool of 7% of company profit. Both also accept the wartime provisions: strategic goods never reach a nation's enemy, trade continues, company assets are protected, and the company takes no side in a war (§11, §12).

## §1 Purpose and Principles

> **What this section does.** It states what the company is for and the principles that every later rule follows. These are that the company is the only seller of company goods, that rewards follow measured contribution rather than reported sales, that all money is in the Credit, that the company is an ordinary business separate from its owners and members, that it is open to every nation with Prunia as its home and takes no side in any war, and that every member can see the ledger, the payments, and the approvals.

**§1.1 Purpose.** The company makes and sells primary goods (raw materials) and secondary goods (processed or crafted items), and may offer services. It pools its members' production so that members can sell at a scale none of them could reach alone, and so that each member's reward follows what that member actually contributes.

**§1.2 One seller.** The company is the only seller of company goods. Members produce and deposit; they do not sell. Because a member never makes the sale, a member cannot under-report one.

**§1.3 Measured contribution.** Rewards follow what physically enters company storage and which assets a member contributes. Both can be checked against chest contents and logs. Rewards never depend on a member's own report of sales.

**§1.4 One currency.** All company banking, prices, payouts, and reimbursements are in the Credit. The company does not convert between currencies unless the law of a nation where it operates requires it.

**§1.5 A business of its own.** The company operates as an ordinary business, separate from its owners and members.

**§1.6 International, with a home.** The company is open to members and customers of any nation. Its home nation is Prunia. It returns value to Prunia and to every nation that signs an agreement with it (§11), and it takes no side in any war beyond the rules in §12.

**§1.7 Openness.** Every member can see the ledger, the payments, and the approvals. The single exception is described in §12.9.

## §3 Membership and the Product Catalog

> **What this section does.** It sets out what members may not sell outside the company, which is what keeps the company the only seller. It defines the catalog and production infrastructure, states the two prohibitions that follow from them, explains how a member may take company goods for personal use, describes how a sale outside the company is proven and penalized, sets how a promotional discount is approved, and allows branded divisions that sell under their own names.

**§3.1 The rule.** A member may not sell outside the company any good on the catalog, and may not sell outside the company any good made with company production infrastructure.

**§3.2 The catalog.** The catalog is a fixed, named list of goods the company officially produces and sells, for example refined iron, redstone components, or a specific crafted item. Anything not on the list is personal activity and is unrestricted. The official catalog is the list of goods published on the company website, which the CEO and the Sales role maintain. Prices shown on the website are for reference only; the price charged in the company's shops governs. A good listed with a bulk quantity is sold at a bulk discount on orders of at least that quantity. The bulk discount is 15% to start, and the CEO or the board may change it like any other approval. It combines with the citizen discount (§11.4) and any promotional discount (§3.7), each applied in turn.

**§3.3 Production infrastructure.** Production infrastructure is any company-owned machine whose output flows automatically into company-owned storage. Goods made this way never pass through a member's inventory.

**§3.4 Two prohibitions.**

1. A member may not sell a catalog good outside the company, however it was produced.
2. A member may not sell outside the company any good made with company production infrastructure, whether or not it is on the catalog.

The second prohibition applies within reason. General-use infrastructure, such as a railroad a member rides, gives the company no claim on items the member happens to be carrying.

**§3.5 Personal use.** A member who wants company-produced goods for personal use takes them through the Sales role at the company's internal price (§2). The ban on resale still applies to those goods.

**§3.6 Enforcement.** Player-to-player transfers are not logged on the server, so a sale outside the company is established by evidence from the buyer or a witness, such as a screenshot of the transfer. Violations are handled under the strike system (§9.8).

**§3.7 Promotional discounts.** A promotional discount is a temporary price reduction on a particular good or service, such as 20% off for two weeks. It is approved like any other approval: the CEO may approve it alone or a majority of the board may approve it, and a majority of the board may challenge the CEO's approval within the 24-hour window (§9.2, §9.3). It also needs the consent of each member who supplies the good or service from production the member owns, such as goods from an asset the member keeps (§7.1) or a service the member provides (§8.4), because that member's share is paid on the reduced price (§4.1). A member who has sold the asset to the company (§7.1), or who only operates or maintains a company-owned asset (§7.7, §7.9), does not need to consent, even if goods the member deposited earlier are still in stock. Once a promotional discount is approved, the Sales role lists it on the company website with its end date, if it has one. It combines with the bulk discount (§3.2) and the citizen discount (§11.4), each applied in turn.

**§3.8 Branded divisions.** A branded division is a part of the company that sells under its own name and logo. It remains part of the company in every respect, and the rules on revenue, supply, equity, dividends, and assets apply to its members exactly as to every other member (§4 to §7).

1. A branded division is created by a 66% vote of the whole board, including the member who will head it (§9.2). The same vote can end it at any time.
2. A good or service carries the division's name when the division produces it and it is listed under that name on the catalog (§3.2), even if company resources helped produce it. Its prices and promotional discounts are set as for any other item (§3.7), and it is sold through company shops, which may display the division's name.
3. The division may use company resources, such as company-owned assets, supplies, or capital, only with the usual approval, and its head may not approve that use. Heading a division gives no powers beyond the member's existing role.
4. The ledger shows the division's revenue separately, for reporting only.
5. A division also ends when its head leaves the company. It does not end because its head is inactive, but once the head has been inactive for six weeks (§6.6), the CEO may end it alone or a majority of the board may end it, and a majority of the board may challenge the CEO's decision within the 24-hour window (§9.2, §9.3). When a division ends, its goods are listed under the company's name, and assets stay with whoever owns them under §7. The division's name and logo belong to its head, and the company stops using them.

## §4 Revenue and Payouts

> **What this section does.** It sets how revenue is shared between a member and the company, and when a member is paid. It covers the 80/20 split and who bears the cost of supplies, the up-front payment on goods with proven revenue and how that status is earned, how goods without it are accepted on consignment, and how the cost of goods relates to dividends.

**§4.1 The split.** A member whose goods are sold through the company receives 80% of the revenue from those goods. The company keeps 20%, unless a separate deal is made. Where a sale carries a discount, such as the bulk discount (§3.2), a promotional discount (§3.7), or the citizen discount (§11.4), the split applies to the discounted price.

**§4.2 Who pays for supplies.** Where a member pays for their own supplies, the split applies to revenue. Where the company pays for approved supplies, the split applies to profit after those supplies (see §7.9).

**§4.3 Up-front payment.** Up-front payment is a benefit of membership: a member is paid without waiting for the sale. It applies only to goods with proven revenue (§4.4), and only while the good is below its target stock (§5.3). When such a good is deposited, the company pays 20% of the member's 80% cut up front, calculated on the good's reference price. When the unit sells, the company pays the rest of the member's cut. The up-front payment is a guarantee. If a unit sells for so little that the member's cut is less than the up-front payment, the member keeps the up-front payment and receives nothing more, and the company bears the difference.

&#91;image: Formula: up-front payment, amount due at sale, and company share\]

**In plain terms.** Here p is the price at which the unit sells and p\_ref is the good's reference price (§4.4). For a good with a reference price of 10 Credits that sells at 10, the member receives 1.6 Credits at deposit and 6.4 Credits at sale, a total of 8, and the company keeps 2.

**§4.4 Proven revenue.** A good has proven revenue once it has sold in at least 3 of the last 4 weeks. Its reference price is the plain average of its average sale price in each of those weeks in which it sold, so every week counts equally however many units sold that week.

**§4.5 Consignment.** A good without proven revenue, and any deposit beyond the amount the company will pay for up front, is accepted on consignment. A consignment deposit is paid only after it sells, at the same 80% share.

**§4.6 Cost of goods and dividends.** What the company pays for deposited goods, the up-front payment plus the amount paid after the sale, is the cost of goods. Dividends are paid from profit after the cost of goods and operating expenses (§6.5).

## §5 Supply Management

> **What this section does.** It decides how much of each good the company will pay for up front each week, how that amount is divided among members, and how the stock is recorded and checked. It covers the target stock for each good, the weekly guarantee budget, the equal entitlements members claim against that budget, the shared pool for entitlements left unclaimed, the stock ledger with its Sales and auditor roles, and the shared log of production.

**§5.1 Up-front payment depends on stock.** Up-front payment is guaranteed only for goods below their target stock. Every other deposit is accepted on consignment (§4.5).

**§5.2 Target stock.** The target stock of a good is two weeks of its expected sales. Target stock and guarantee budgets are set for each good across all regions together (§12.2). A new server has no sales history, so starting values are fixed and conservative, then adjusted from the ledger.

```text
T_g = τ × D_g,   where τ = 2
```

**In plain terms.** For good g, T\_g is the target stock, D\_g is the expected weekly sell-through, and τ is the number of weeks of cover the company aims to hold, currently 2.

**§5.3 Guarantee budget.** The weekly guarantee budget for a good is the volume the company will pay for up front that week. It is the target stock minus the stock already held, and never less than zero.

&#91;image: Formula: guarantee budget equals the larger of zero and the target stock minus the stock on hand\]

**In plain terms.** V\_g is the guaranteed volume of good g and S\_g is the stock currently held. If expected weekly sales are 60 units and 30 units are in stock, the target is 120, so the budget is 90 units. If stock reaches or passes the target, the budget is zero and further deposits go on consignment.

**§5.4 Entitlements.** The budget is divided into entitlements, one per active member. Entitlements are equal, so guaranteed revenue does not depend on equity, and contributing more goods earns no larger entitlement. If a good has a contract holder (§8.1), the holder's committed quota is covered first and the rest is divided equally among the other active members.

&#91;image: Formula: entitlement of each member, with and without a contract holder\]

**In plain terms.** Here q is a member's entitlement in units, n is the number of active members, h is the contract holder, and c\_g is the holder's committed quota. With a budget of 120 units and 4 active members, each member's entitlement is 30. If one member holds a contract with a quota of 50, that member's entitlement is 50 and each of the other three has (120 − 50) ÷ 3 ≈ 23.3.

**§5.5 Claims and the shared pool.** A member may claim an entitlement at any time during the week. Entitlements still unclaimed 48 hours before the weekly reset move to a shared pool, where claims are first come, first served. A deposit beyond a member's entitlement and the pool is accepted on consignment.

**§5.6 The stock ledger and the roles that keep it.** The company keeps a ledger recording, for each good, how much each member has contributed, how much remains in stock, and how much has been sold. It supplies the demand and stock figures used in §5.3.

1. The Sales role records every deposit and withdrawal when it happens. A contributor never records their own deposit.
2. With fewer than 5 eligible members, one member holds the Sales role, hand deposits are recorded in front of a second member whenever one is online, and the board reviews the ledger every two weeks.
3. From 5 eligible members, a separate auditor role takes effect. Nobody holds both roles, and nobody audits their own entries. Each week the auditor compares the ledger with the actual chest contents for a random sample of goods, and any discrepancy goes to the board.
4. The CEO appoints both roles from eligible members. Roles rotate automatically every 8 weeks, and the board or the CEO can reappoint a holder.
5. Role holders receive a labor fee, set for each case when the role is approved.
6. Deliberately falsifying an entry is a severe offense (§9.9).

**§5.7 Visibility of production.** A shared log shows what is being produced and in what volume. Members can see when a good is well covered and may choose to specialize elsewhere. No member is ordered to.

## §6 Equity and Dividends

> **What this section does.** It explains how members earn a stake in the company, how profit is shared, and what happens to a stake when a member is inactive or leaves. It covers how equity points are earned, shared out, and boosted for early contributions, how a member is paid for an asset, how dividends are set and split, the activity test that decides who counts as active, lull periods, the decay of inactive points, and the buyback when a member leaves.

**§6.1 Equity points.** Equity comes only from assets contributed to the company, valued at set prices (§7.4). Time counts only where it builds a company-owned asset, priced at an imputed labor rate. Points accumulate from contributions, and only the part of a price taken as equity earns points (§6.4, §7.10). There is no cap on points.

**§6.2 Equity share.** A member's equity share is the member's points divided by the total points of all active members. Inactive members are left out of the total.

&#91;image: Formula: a member's equity share equals activity-weighted equity points divided by the total\]

**In plain terms.** E\_i is member i's points, and a\_i is 1 for an active member and 0 for an inactive one. A member with 300 points in a company whose active members hold 1,000 points in total has a share of 0.3.

**§6.3 Early-contribution multiplier.** An asset contributed early earns more points than the same asset contributed later, because assets are harder to obtain early. The multiplier is 1.5 at server launch, falls in a straight line to 1.0 at six weeks, and stays at 1.0 afterward.

&#91;image: Formula: early-contribution multiplier, starting at 1.5 and falling in a straight line to 1 at six weeks\]

**In plain terms.** Here t is the number of weeks since server launch. At week 3 the multiplier is 1.25. It never falls below 1, so a contribution always keeps its full base value.

**§6.4 Payment for assets.** A member who sells an asset to the company chooses the form of payment: all equity, part equity and part cash or goods, or all cash or goods. Only the equity portion earns points.

&#91;image: Formula: equity points earned for an asset, and the cash or goods paid for the remainder\]

**In plain terms.** Here E is the equity points earned, v is the asset's set value (§7.4), m(t) is the early-contribution multiplier at week t (§6.3), and φ is the fraction of that value the member takes as equity. A member who sells an asset worth 1,000 Credits at week 3 and takes half as equity receives 500 Credits and 500 × 1.25 = 625 points. Guaranteed revenue is paid for goods sold to the company, and equity is paid for assets sold to the company. The two are separate rewards.

**§6.5 Dividends.** Only part of profit is distributed. The rest stays in the company to fund growth. The rate at server launch is 25% of profit. After that, the rate is changed like any other approval: the CEO may change it alone or a majority of the board may change it, and a majority of the board may challenge the CEO's change within the 24-hour window (§9.2, §9.3). Of the amount distributed, 85% is paid by equity share and 15% is divided equally among active members. A new member who holds equity receives both portions from the first week. A new member without equity receives no part of the equal portion, and is not counted in dividing it, until the first two weeks of membership have passed. Inactive members receive nothing, including the equal portion.

&#91;image: Formula: dividend paid to a member, 85 percent by equity and 15 percent equally\]

**In plain terms.** D\_i is member i's dividend, Π is profit (§2), ρ is the share of profit distributed (0.25 at server launch, and set by the CEO or the board afterward), a\_i is 1 for a member who shares the equal portion and 0 otherwise, and n is the number of members who share it: every active member except a new member without equity in the first two weeks. With a profit of 1,000, 250 is distributed. With 4 active members, a member whose equity share is 0.4 receives 250 × (0.85 × 0.4 + 0.15 ÷ 4) = 94.375 Credits.

**§6.6 Activity test.** A member passes the activity test by making a minimum deposit or completing a company task within the trailing two weeks. A new member counts as active for the first two weeks after joining, and must then pass the test like any other member. A new member without equity does not share in the equal portion of the dividend during those two weeks (§6.5). If a good has no target stock, for example because it has no sales history, the CEO or the board may set a target for it under the usual approval and challenge rules (§9.2, §9.3), and deposits of that good then count toward the test against that target.

&#91;image: Formula: activity test, the sum of deposits relative to target stock must reach half of an equal share\]

**In plain terms.** Here x is the amount of good g that member i deposited over the past two weeks and T\_g is that good's target stock (§5.2). The member must deposit, summed across goods, at least half of an equal share of one target stock. The board may adjust the 0.5. Until sales history exists, the board sets a fixed starting amount, or members pass by completing a task.

A member who has not passed the test for two weeks is inactive. Inactive members' points stop counting toward the total, and they receive no dividends. Their points are kept, not deleted, and count again once the member is active again. Entitlements (§5.4) go to active members only.

**§6.7 Lull periods.** The board may declare a lull period by a 66% vote. It pauses the activity and decay clocks for its duration.

**§6.8 Decay of inactive points.** The points of an inactive member begin to decay six weeks after the member's last activity. The decay is in a straight line and stops at a floor of 50% at 12 weeks of inactivity. Decayed points are permanently lost when the member returns.

&#91;image: Formula: decay factor falling from 1 to a floor of 0.5 between 6 and 12 weeks of inactivity\]

**In plain terms.** Here u is the number of weeks since the member's last activity, and d is the share of the points that remains. At 6 weeks or fewer, d is 1. At 9 weeks, d is 0.75. At 12 weeks or more, d is 0.5.

**§6.9 Leaving and buyback.** A member who leaves is removed from the point total. The company buys back the member's equity at its original grant value, which is the equity portion of each asset's value when it was sold, without the early-contribution bonus, multiplied by the member's decay factor at departure.

```text
Buyback = d × Σ (φ_k × v_k),   summed over every asset k the member sold
```

**In plain terms.** For each asset k the member sold, φ\_k is the equity fraction and v\_k is the set value. A departing member does not share in growth beyond the payouts already received. Payment may be in goods, money, or other agreed conditions, and goods are valued at the internal price (§2). Buyback counts toward the weekly cash limit (§7.6), any excess is paid in installments over later weeks, and the 66% board override applies. A member removed for wrongdoing receives a buyback reduced in proportion to the reason for removal.

## §7 Assets and Ownership

> **What this section does.** It explains whether a member keeps an asset or sells it to the company, how an asset's value is set, how builders of infrastructure are paid, and who pays for supplies. It also covers the approval needed before building, the weekly cash limit on payments for assets, the operator share of margin, maintenance and supplies, how a sale is paid in stages, and builds whose goods have no sales record yet.

**§7.1 Two options.** A member's asset is either kept by the member or sold to the company. The default is that the member keeps it. The company buys an asset only when it needs the asset strategically, and only by approval.

|  | Member-owned (default) | Company-owned (by approval) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it works | The member keeps the asset and sells its goods through the company, 80% to the member and 20% to the company. | The company buys an asset the member built with personal capital. |
| Advantage to the member | Keeps 80% of revenue for as long as the asset runs, which over the long term pays more than a sale price. Receives the up-front payment on proven goods. Keeps control. Sells at a scale a member could not reach alone, using the company's storefronts, infrastructure, and connections. | Receives the purchase price, which frees capital for the next build. May take payment as equity, which shares in company profit and earns the early-contribution multiplier. An approved build is paid whether or not it makes money. |
| Advantage to the company | Earns 20% of the revenue with no capital outlay. Supply of catalog goods grows faster, and the single-seller rule holds. | Controls supply and keeps it running if the owner leaves or goes inactive. Can run assets too large for one member. Keeps the full margin apart from the operator share. |
| Limit | The company has no control over the asset. If the member stops operating it or leaves, supply ends. | Costs the company capital. Requires approval, and the value is fixed at approval. The member gives up the 80% long-term return. |

**§7.2 Keeping versus selling.** Selling an asset pays less than keeping it once the asset has run long enough.

```text
θ > H / (0.8 − ω)
```

**In plain terms.** Here θ is the number of weeks the asset runs, H is the number of weeks of the asset's margin paid up front at purchase (4), and ω is the operator share of margin after a sale (0.2 to start). With those values, keeping pays more on cash alone once the asset runs for more than about 6.7 weeks. Equity, the early-contribution multiplier, and freed capital are the reasons to sell.

**§7.3 Approval and funding.**

1. An asset's value reflects how useful it is to the company, including demand for what it produces. It does not reflect what the member spent building it, because cost-based pricing would reward padding a build with extra expense.
2. A member needs approval from the CEO or the board before building in order to be guaranteed that the company will buy the asset. Without approval, the company owes no purchase and no compensation.
3. A member may not build with company capital without approval from the CEO or the board.
4. The company buys an asset only if the member built it with personal capital. A member who builds using company-owned goods and infrastructure is compensated for time only (§7.5).

**§7.4 Valuation.** An asset's value is set at approval and is not recalculated afterward. The margin-based valuation below applies only to assets that produce goods with proven revenue, and it guides the figure.

&#91;image: Formula: value of a delivery of goods\]

**In plain terms.** Here m\_g is the margin per unit of good g, which is the profit a unit earns after its supplies. The letter q is the asset's weekly output, D\_g is expected weekly demand, and Q\_g is the weekly supply that already exists. Only output that meets unmet demand counts. For a margin of 2 Credits per unit, an output of 50 units a week, demand of 100, and existing supply of 70, the unmet demand is 30, so v = 2 × 30 × 4 = 240 Credits.

**§7.5 Time and mixed funding.** A member who builds with company-owned goods and infrastructure receives a fixed labor fee. It equals an imputed labor rate times the approved build effort, not reported hours. The labor rate is decided for each case at approval. If a build uses both personal and company capital, the member is paid the personal-capital share of the asset's value plus the labor fee.

**§7.6 Cash limit.** Cash paid for assets in any week may not exceed 25% of the treasury balance. Any excess is paid as equity or queued, unless a 66% majority of the board approves a larger cash payment. No reserve floor limits that override. Buybacks (§6.9) count toward the same limit. Reimbursements (§10) do not.

**§7.7 Operator share.** For a company-owned asset, the operator receives a share of the asset's margin. The starting share is 20%. It is lowered for assets that need almost no labor and raised for labor-intensive ones.

**§7.8 Infrastructure and weekly payments.** A member who builds infrastructure for the company is paid in equity for the contribution, or in cash or goods if the member chooses, as with any asset. The member also receives weekly payments based on how valuable the infrastructure is to the company.

1. The weekly payment applies to infrastructure that supports the company without earning its own margin, such as rails, storage, and defense. Assets that produce goods earn the operator share instead.
2. The CEO or the board sets the weekly payment at approval, based on the infrastructure's value to the company. It is guaranteed for the first two weeks.
3. After that the board reviews it every two weeks. A 66% vote can raise it by any amount, lower it by at most 25% per review, or retire the infrastructure and stop the payment if it is obsolete. The builder does not vote on the review. Changes apply going forward.
4. The builder must maintain the infrastructure, using company capital once the CEO or the board approves. The builder may request additional compensation for further work, which must be approved with the payments decided beforehand.
5. Each week's payment is scaled by that week's company revenue against its four-week average, so the company does not pay a premium during server lulls.

&#91;image: Formula: weekly infrastructure payment scaled by revenue\]

**In plain terms.** Here R\_w is the company's revenue for the week, R̄ (R-bar) is its average over the previous four weeks, and f is the floor of 0.5. If the week's revenue is 60% of the average, the payment is 60% of the approved amount. If revenue is 30% of the average, the payment is 50%. If revenue is at or above the average, the full amount is paid. This scaling is separate from the 25% limit on review cuts, which governs changes to the approved amount itself.

**§7.9 Maintenance and supplies.** The member who maintains an asset is responsible for keeping it stocked and running. The company pays for supplies once the CEO or the board approves. For repetitive supply, the CEO or the board may pre-approve a budget for a good whose supply needs are known. The budget must be spent on those supplies and cannot be kept. Keeping it counts as defrauding the company, which is a severe offense (§9.9). The weekly maintenance payment is scaled by actual output against approved output, so an understocked or broken asset pays less automatically, whatever the cause.

&#91;image: Formula: weekly maintenance payment scaled by revenue\]

**In plain terms.** An asset that produces 80% of its approved output is paid 80% of its base amount. One that produces more than its approved output is paid the base amount, not more. Rails, storage, and defense have no output to measure, so the CEO or the board confirms each week that they are working.

**§7.10 Staged payment.** When the company buys an asset, it pays half of the price at purchase and the rest as installments out of the margin the asset actually earns, capped at the price. If the member takes part of the price as equity (§6.4), the equity is paid in the same stages: each payment, at purchase and from each installment, is split between equity and cash or goods in the proportion the member chose. The equity part earns points at the early-contribution multiplier in effect on the date of purchase (§6.3).

```text
At purchase = α × v,   where α = 0.5
```

**In plain terms.** Here v is the asset's value and α is the share paid at purchase, 0.5 to start. For an asset valued at 1,000 Credits, 500 is paid at purchase. The other 500 is paid in installments from the asset's earned margin, and the installments never total more than the price.

**§7.11 Unproven goods.** If the asset's good does not have proven revenue, the company can still buy the asset, but it pays no price for revenue the asset might generate. Instead the member receives a larger share of the asset's revenue for two weeks, after which the normal operator share applies. The member is paid whether or not the asset makes money, so the company bears the risk. This applies only to builds approved beforehand, and only the first member to launch a good qualifies.

1. For an approved build, the fixed payment is the approved capital cost, capped by the approved budget, paid in the form the member chooses. The larger share is 50% of the asset's revenue for the first two weeks, or 50% of its margin where the company pays the asset's supplies.
2. For a build made without approval, a good with proven revenue is valued normally with the staged payment. For a good without proven revenue, no fixed payment is made. The company either waits until the good is proven, or the member accepts the two-week larger share and then the operator share, and bears the risk.

## §8 Contracts and Services

> **What this section does.** It covers special arrangements: priority contracts that let a member's goods sell first, goods outside the catalog, custom orders, and services. It sets the quota a contract holder commits to and when a contract lapses, who owns a custom good depending on whose materials built it, and how a service is booked, held in escrow, confirmed, and paid.

**§8.1 Priority contracts.** The company may make a specific deal under which a member's goods are the first drawn from stock for sale, ahead of other members' units of the same good. The contract holder is credited with those sales until the holder's units run out. After that, the remaining quota is filled through entitlements and the shared pool (§5.4, §5.5). In return, the holder commits to a weekly quota.

```text
c_g = κ × D_g,   where κ = 0.5 to start
```

**In plain terms.** The committed quota c\_g is a fixed percentage κ of the good's expected weekly demand D\_g. For a good with weekly demand of 100 units, a quota of 50% is 50 units a week.

1. Priority is capped at the committed quota, so the rest of demand still sells from other members' units.
2. A shortfall reverts to normal selling for that good. A contract lapses if the holder misses the quota two weeks in a row.
3. A good has at most one contract holder.
4. Contract holders do not sell outside the company, so the company remains the only seller.
5. A contract governs the order in which units sell. Entitlements govern up-front payment.

**§8.2 Unorthodox goods.** Goods outside the normal catalog, such as a specialized logistics business, may receive specialized contracts and deals. Builders of infrastructure that such a business depends on are paid under §7.8.

**§8.3 Custom buyer orders.** This clause covers intermediate goods and custom orders. Goods that are not on the catalog are unrestricted.

1. A member who makes one from personal materials may sell it anywhere. The member may also route it through the company at the usual split of 80% to the member and 20% to the company, with the job recorded in the ledger.
2. If all the materials and infrastructure came from the company, the good belongs to the company, which keeps the proceeds after supplies. The member receives a fixed labor fee set for the case at approval.
3. If the member supplies part of the materials, the member receives the value of those materials at the internal price (§2) plus the labor fee, and the company keeps the rest.
4. A buyer who orders from the company gets a quote from the Sales role, pays into escrow, and has the 48-hour dispute window described for services in §8.4. The buyer may collect the order at a company store or have it delivered, at a delivery rate quoted by a board member.

**§8.4 Services.** A service is work done for a buyer, for example a delivery or a build. Services cannot be deposited like goods, so the company contracts each job.

1. The buyer books through the company, which quotes a price from the published service schedule, the services section of the company website. As with goods, the price charged at booking governs. Custom jobs are quoted and approved by the Sales role.
2. The buyer pays the company at booking. The company holds the payment in escrow, meaning it keeps the money in trust, until the job is done.
3. The Sales role records each job in the ledger (provider, buyer, service, price, completion date). The provider never records their own job.
4. On completion, the provider receives 80% and the company keeps 20%. Where the provider uses company-owned infrastructure, its builder is paid under §7.8.
5. A job counts as confirmed if the buyer does not dispute it within 48 hours of the provider reporting it complete. The board settles disputes.

## §9 Governance and Enforcement

> **What this section does.** It says who makes decisions, how members can challenge or remove those who lead, and what happens to a member who breaks the rules. It covers the board and its powers, which approvals the CEO can give alone and which need the board, the 24-hour challenge window, who may vote, how the CEO can be removed, the Sales and auditor roles, who can see the ledger, the three-strike system, and severe offenses.

**§9.1 The board.** The CEO establishes the board and is a member of it. The CEO appoints board members, and a majority vote of eligible members can remove a board member. A board member does not vote on a payment that goes to that member. Removing the CEO follows §9.5.

**§9.2 Approvals.**

1. The CEO alone can approve builds, contracts, supply budgets, changes to the bulk discount (§3.2), promotional discounts (§3.7), and changes to the dividend rate (§6.5). A majority of the board can challenge such an approval before or after the CEO makes it, and can also approve these on its own, without the CEO. A majority means more than half of the board, and a tie leaves the CEO's approval standing.
2. A 66% vote of the board is required for cash-limit overrides (§7.6), changes to infrastructure payments (§7.8), lull periods (§6.7), decisions on severe offenses (§9.9), wartime remedies beyond escrow (§12.8), creating or ending a branded division, except that one whose head has been inactive for six weeks may be ended under §3.8, and any payment to the CEO.
3. An approval that benefits the CEO personally needs a majority of the board excluding the CEO.

**§9.3 Challenge window.** The challenge window is 24 hours after the CEO's approval. Members may start building before it closes, at the risk of the approval being blocked. After the window closes, the approval is final, and the board can change only future decisions.

**§9.4 Votes and eligibility.** Every member vote is one member, one vote, and is not weighted by equity, except for the CEO removal vote (§9.5). A new member has no vote and no petition right until the member has been active for 8 weeks and has passed the activity test (§6.6). Members who have done so are eligible members.

**§9.5 Removing the CEO.**

1. A petition from one-third of eligible members, and at least 3 members, can call a removal vote.
2. Removal requires 66% of eligible members and also a majority of equity, with the CEO's own equity left out of the count. This prevents new members, who have no equity, from removing the CEO alone.
3. After a failed vote, no new removal vote can be called for 8 weeks.

**§9.6 Roles.** The Sales role and the auditor role are described in §5.6. The CEO appoints both, they rotate every 8 weeks, and nobody holds both at once.

**§9.7 Visibility.** All members can see the ledger, the payments, and the approvals. The single exception is in §12.9.

**§9.8 Violations and strikes.** A violation of the outside-sale rule (§3.1) is established by evidence from a buyer or witness (§3.6). A member receives three strikes with graduated penalties and is removed at the fourth.

| Strike | Penalty |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | The member repays the company's 20% share of the undeclared sale and receives a written warning. |
| 2 | The member repays the entire sale to the company. |
| 3 | The member repays the entire sale, and the member's equity points are frozen for four weeks. |
| 4 | Removal, with the buyback (§6.9) reduced in proportion to the offense. |

Strikes expire after 12 weeks without a violation.

**§9.9 Severe offenses.** Severe offenses are a separate category from selling outside the books and are handled separately from strikes. They are deliberately falsifying a ledger entry, defrauding the company of money, and harming other members. The board decides the outcome by a 66% vote, with the accused member not voting. The buyback is reduced in proportion to the offense.

## §10 Member-Advanced Purchases

> **What this section does.** It guarantees that a member who pays with personal money for an approved company purchase, because no board member is online, is repaid within 7 days. It sets the conditions the purchase must meet, the priority of reimbursements over other payments, the premium owed when the company pays late, and the treatment of false claims.

**§10.1 The rule.** When a member has permission to buy something with company money but no board member is online to make the transaction, the member may pay with their own money. The company guarantees to reimburse the member within 7 days, counted from the date the goods are recorded in company storage.

**§10.2 Conditions.** The guarantee applies only if all of the following hold.

1. A recorded approval covers the purchase. It names the item, a maximum unit price, a maximum quantity, and the purchaser. Approvals are not retroactive, and a purchase made without one carries no guarantee.
2. No board member is online when the member buys, and the member has first posted the request in the board's channel. The purchase is logged with a timestamp.
3. The member provides proof: a shop-plugin log entry, or a screenshot with a second member as witness. A player-to-player purchase without a witness is reimbursed only at the board's discretion, because transfers between players are not logged.
4. The goods are deposited in company storage and recorded by the Sales role. The purchase counts as a completed company task for the activity test (§6.6), but not as a deposit and not toward equity points, because company money paid for the goods.
5. An approval above 10% of the treasury goes through the 24-hour challenge window (§9.3) before the member spends. Smaller approvals may be spent immediately.

**§10.3 Priority.** Reimbursements are paid ahead of dividends, buybacks, and new asset purchases. They are not subject to the weekly cash limit (§7.6), because they settle an approved obligation.

**§10.4 Late payment.** If the company pays after the 7-day window, it adds a premium of 10% of the claim for each full week of delay, up to 30%.

&#91;image: Formula: reimbursement equals the lesser of the amount paid and the approved cap, plus a premium for late payment\]

**In plain terms.** Repay is the amount the company pays. The amount p\_paid is what the member actually paid. The amount p\_cap is the approved maximum unit price times the quantity bought, up to the approved quantity. The number L is the count of full weeks the payment is late after the 7-day window ends. The company never repays more than the approved cap. If a member paid 900 Credits against an approved cap of 1,000 and is repaid two full weeks late, the member receives 900 × 1.2 = 1,080 Credits. A payment five weeks late would carry the maximum premium of 30%. No interest or other premium applies.

**§10.5 False claims.** An inflated or false claim counts as defrauding the company, which is a severe offense (§9.9).

## §11 Nations: the Home Charter and the Stetheros Compact

> **What this section does.** It sets out how the company returns value to the nations where it operates. Prunia, the home nation, is covered by the Home Charter, and any other nation may sign the Stetheros Compact. It lists what each side gives and receives under the two agreements, the 10% citizen discount and how it interacts with the revenue split, the profit pool and how it is shared among nations, and the rule against reselling discounted goods.

**§11.1 Two agreements.** Both agreements are contracts between the company and a nation's government. Any nation that grants benefits similar to those Prunia grants can receive similar extras. The one exception is priority on stock that is not tied to any nation, which belongs only to the home nation, because the company cannot give that priority to both sides of a war (§12.4). The company is a private business with Prunia as its home jurisdiction, and it does not take any nation's side in a war beyond the rules in §12.

**§11.2 The Home Charter (Prunia).**

Prunia gives the company:

1. favorable economic deals with its government;
2. first pick when the company buys resources;
3. a reduced sales tax;
4. compensation for foreign-currency exchange costs while the company's stores promote the Credit;
5. acceptance of the wartime provisions (§12).

The company gives Prunia:

1. banking only in the Credit (§1.4);
2. a 10% goods discount for the government and citizens (§11.4);
3. a first right to buy goods and services ahead of nations outside the Home Charter and the Stetheros Compact, applying when stock is short, at the normal price;
4. first call on strategic goods produced in Prunia, and on stock that is not tied to any nation (§12.4);
5. a share of the profit pool (§11.5).

Two further terms are *provisional*:

1. The Prunian treasury should receive at least as much from the company as it pays out, measured each period by the formula below.
2. The company will not move its core infrastructure and storage out of Prunia without Prunian consent.

```text
N = Taxes + Pool − Exchange ≥ 0
```

**In plain terms.** Taxes are what the company pays Prunia in tax. Pool is the Prunian share of the profit pool (§11.5). Exchange is the exchange-cost compensation that Prunia pays the company. The test passes when taxes plus the pool share are at least the exchange compensation. With taxes of 100, a pool share of 150, and exchange compensation of 40, the result is 210, so the test passes.

**§11.3 The Stetheros Compact (any nation).** A signatory nation receives:

1. a 10% discount on company goods for its government and citizens, including strategic goods, the same discount the Home Charter grants Prunia (§11.4);
2. a first right to buy goods and services ahead of nations outside the Compact, applying when stock is short, at the normal price;
3. a share of the profit pool (§11.5).

The company receives from a signatory nation:

1. a commitment that buyers and suppliers for the company in the nation use the Credit;
2. a cut in the nation's sales tax on company sales equal to 20% of its existing rate (*provisional*);
3. first pick when the company buys assets in the nation's region;
4. a citizen registry, so that the discount reaches the right buyers;
5. acceptance of the wartime provisions (§12).

```text
r_new = 0.8 × r_old
```

**In plain terms.** The new sales tax rate is 80% of the existing rate. A nation whose sales tax is 10% would lower it to 8%. The size of the cut therefore depends on each nation's existing rate.

**§11.4 The citizen discount.** The government and citizens of every nation under the Home Charter or the Stetheros Compact receive a 10% discount on company goods, including strategic goods. There is no cap on discounted purchases. The discount comes off the whole sale price first, and the 80/20 split (§4.1) is then applied to the discounted price. On goods the company produces itself, the company bears the whole discount.

&#91;image: Formula: member and company amounts after the citizen discount\]

**In plain terms.** Here p is the list price and d is the discount rate. For a good listed at 100 Credits, the buyer pays 90. The member receives 72 and the company keeps 18, so the discount is shared between them in proportion to their shares.

**§11.5 The profit pool.** The company sets aside 7% of its profit for the pool nations, which are Prunia and every signatory nation. Each nation's share is half its portion of the value of company-owned infrastructure and half its portion of company sales, measured by where the assets and shops are located, not by the nationality of members or the origin of the stock sold. The pool is calculated and paid each dividend period on the same profit figure as the dividend, and it is zero when profit is zero or negative. The pool and the dividend are each calculated on that full profit figure, and neither is deducted before the other is calculated.

&#91;image: Formula: nation profit pool allocation, half by infrastructure value and half by sales\]

**In plain terms.** P\_r is nation r's payment and Π is total company profit for the period. A\_r is the approved value of company-owned infrastructure currently active in nation r, and retired infrastructure is excluded. S\_r is the revenue the company received from sales at shops in nation r during the period, after discounts. Each sum runs over every pool nation m, Prunia included. If either total is zero, the other term carries the full weight. A new signatory dilutes the other nations' shares rather than enlarging the pool. For example, suppose Prunia holds 80% of the infrastructure value and 70% of the sales, and a second nation holds 20% and 30%. The Prunian weight is 0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.7 = 0.75 and the second nation's is 0.25. On a profit of 1,000 Credits the pool is 70, of which Prunia receives 52.5 and the second nation 17.5.

**§11.6 Resale of discounted goods.** Discounted goods may be resold or passed on only to the governments and citizens of nations under the Home Charter or the Stetheros Compact, who qualify for the discount anyway. Resale to anyone else is prohibited. The company acts on any prohibited resale under the rules below.

1. A violation is established by evidence from the buyer or a witness of the resale itself, such as a screenshot of the resale. A majority of the board decides. Ordinary purchases from the company need no documentation.
2. The penalties escalate. The values below are *provisional*.

| Violation | Penalty |
| --- | --- |
| First | The buyer loses the discount for 4 weeks and receives a written warning. |
| Second | The buyer loses the discount for 12 weeks. |
| Third | The buyer loses the discount permanently. |

3. A nation commits to cooperate and to act against officials who run resale schemes. Repeated violations by a nation's buyers or officials go up the remedy ladder in §12.8.

## §12 Wartime Provisions

> **What this section does.** It keeps the company whole if nations go to war. Every signatory stays supplied, strategic goods never reach an opponent of the nation that produced them, and company assets are protected. It covers where goods are stored and how they are tagged, the strategic goods rule and priority on strategic goods, continued trade and embargoes, protection of assets, escrow as a bond, the ladder of remedies, what information is restricted, and leave for members during a war.

**§12.1 Scope and purpose.** Both the Home Charter and the Stetheros Compact include these provisions. Under them, a signatory may not seize assets in its territory. A nation that feels cut off, or sees company assets helping its opponent, would otherwise be tempted to take them. The provisions therefore keep every signatory supplied, keep strategic goods from reaching an opponent, and make breaking them cost more than it gains. The server's war system has no declared enemies, so who counts as an enemy is determined dynamically, from the war system's own record of a war.

**§12.2 Regional stock.** Goods are stored in the region where they are produced and are tagged with their origin. They are moved only to be sold, except where the board directs that certain goods be held in a particular region. The origin tag stays with the goods wherever they are moved, and the rules in §12.3 and §12.4 follow the tag, not the current location. Regional stock does not change target stock or guarantee budgets, which are set for each good across all regions together (§5.2).

**§12.3 Strategic goods.** Which goods are strategic is determined dynamically, according to the circumstances of each war. For goods that are:

1. Strategic goods produced in a nation's region are never sold to that nation's enemy.
2. A nation at war may buy strategic goods only if they were produced in regions that exclude its enemy.
3. A buyer may not pass company strategic goods on to a party at war with the nation where they were produced.
4. Violations are established by evidence and handled under §12.8.

**§12.4 Priority.** Each nation has first call on the strategic goods produced in its own region. Prunia also has first call on stock that is not tied to any nation.

**§12.5 Trade continues.** Trade continues unless a government with jurisdiction forbids it. If Prunia declares an embargo, the company follows it, but the embargo covers only strategic goods and sales to the belligerent's government. Civilian goods continue to every signatory at the normal price, with citizen discounts. No signatory is cut off completely.

**§12.6 Assets.** A signatory agrees not to seize, freeze, or tax in a discriminatory way any company or member assets in its territory, whatever the owner's nationality. If requisition is unavoidable, the nation pays the set value in the Credit. The highest war level on the server lets a winner take anything, so, as a *provisional* term, company-owned storage and infrastructure are to be exempted from capture in the war system itself.

**§12.7 Escrow as a bond.** During a war, pool payments (§11.5) to a belligerent signatory are held in escrow, meaning the company keeps them in trust. If the nation seizes assets, the escrow offsets the loss. Otherwise it is released after the war.

**§12.8 Remedies.** The ladder has three steps: a written warning, then escrow, then suspension of Compact benefits. Any step beyond escrow needs a 66% vote of the board and a written reason shown to all members. Civilian trade is never cut off completely.

**§12.9 Information.** Sales of strategic goods to governments appear in the ledger as an aggregate figure. The detail is visible only to the board and the auditor. This is the one exception to the rule that all members see the ledger (§9.7). A nation also agrees not to use its access to company records for military intelligence.

**§12.10 Wartime leave.** On request during a war, a member may take wartime leave. For the first six weeks of leave, the member's activity and decay clocks (§6.6, §6.8) pause, so the member stays active, keeps receiving dividends, and the member's points do not decay. After six weeks, the member becomes inactive and receives no dividends (§6.5), but the decay clock stays paused for the rest of the war. Leave ends when the member returns to work by passing the activity test. The company buys back the equity of a member on leave under §6.9, with no decay applied, only if the member formally leaves the company or has not returned within two weeks after the war ends. No member is required to produce or handle strategic goods. As a *provisional* term, role holders from a belligerent nation step aside from strategic orders only.
